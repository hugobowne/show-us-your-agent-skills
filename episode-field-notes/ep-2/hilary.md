Hilary Mason is CEO/co-founder of Hidden Door, an AI-powered game and interactive storytelling platform where players collaborate with AI characters in world-building, character creation, and live roleplaying scenarios. Previously founded Fast Forward Labs (acquired by Cloudera) and served as chief scientist at Bitly. ## Workflows

### Interview-then-test-then-iterate for creative specifications
Hidden Door's core workflow for refinement, lifted from agentic software practices into creative domains. Steps: (1) Interview the person doing the work from five different angles to pin down what they actually want, not what they say they want. (2) Establish current state by running tests against frozen game states or other creative outputs. (3) Generate multiple variations (see skill above). (4) Build a robust eval rubric with editorial criteria. (5) Score each variation against the rubric and compare to baseline to see if you improved or regressed. Hilary: *"This is the idea behind our adaptation of sort of agentic software workflows to creative workflows. And we have a bunch of places we use the philosophy, but it's essentially first interview the person doing the work. What do they actually want? And this is not trust them when they say something. It is ask questions to come at it from five different directions."* [01:01:00–01:01:48]

### Eval-driven creative refinement (goal-aligned, not binary)
The evals don't hand down a pass/fail; they establish a baseline and let you know if you moved the needle. Hilary: *"we define evals as sort of like a, you know, here is a set of criteria that we are looking for editorially. Compare each output against this set of criteria, score it, run multiple variations, and therefore, you know, we get to something that is not-- it's not going to give you an independent sense of like, this is great, but it will give you something you can compare to that eval we did at the beginning of the whole process so that you know you have made something better or you have made something worse."* The same process works for software prompts, content generation, and character refinement. [01:03:16–01:03:28]

### Ask for multiple variations in a single prompt
A meta-pattern Hilary uses everywhere: when asking an LLM for creative alternatives, request three distinct versions at once (with different magnitudes of change and risk) rather than asking for one and re-prompting. *"if you ask it at once to give you three very different versions that have, uh, different magnitudes of change and risk in the changes, you can actually get multiple variations that are somewhat more creative than if you g- you ask for just one."* She's experimented up to ten; three is the sweet spot for most problems, five for more experimental work. Her one-thing-to-take-away: *"if anyone is like taking one thing away for creative stuff, multiple variations."* [01:02:23–01:02:48]

That is some of the LLM tasks that we use to, um, monitor stories in the background."* [01:04:05]

### Pre-compute high-latency outputs, walk through canned results live
The underlying principle: separate computation from presentation when runtime is non-deterministic. Hilary ran her test story task at 5am while jet-lagged (latency dropped sharply, output in six minutes twenty-three seconds), then walked through the canned results on stream. Same logic applies to demos, customer-facing presentations, and course material — anywhere variable agent latency would derail the room. *"when I, uh, ran this early this morn- I just got back from Europe, so I'm suitably jet lagged, and I was running it at, like, 5:00 AM Eastern or something, and it was lightning fast. It's the best time."* [01:09:55–01:14:59]

### Time-bounded exploratory R&D
Between meetings, with spare tokens, Hilary takes 30-minute windows to pursue half-baked ideas (typos into packages, sound effects, weird tangents). Not a formal workflow but a philosophy: use agents to make exploration cheap enough that bad ideas are worth shipping. Hilary: *"I am, uh, I'm the champion of, like, um, use the tech to explore the space of what is possibly interesting, and if you only, like... If you show me what's on your roadmap and it's, like, only good obvious ideas, it is very boring. So just, like, push those a little bit in a creative way."* [01:12:24–01:14:59]

### Hidden Door's internal gremlins
A weekly scheduled agent system inspired by the Nightshift library. Three gremlin personalities (herself, a perfectionist focused on code/product quality, and a player/user-advocate perspective) autonomously pull ideas from Hidden Door's Bad Ideas Slack channel, refine them against their assigned goals, pitch and critique each other, and write design docs proposing next work. Runs every Sunday night as a cron job; team reviews results Monday morning. *"I love them. So I was inspired by Nightshift, which I don't know if you know it, but it's a library that checks if you have any extra tokens in your subscription budget at the end of the week, and then it does all the things you're supposed to do as, like, a really good software engineer."* What she wanted instead: *"it's the end of the week, I have some tokens. Um, but what I want is to work on the weird stuff, like all the bad ideas, all the stuff that, like, nobody has the time for because the, it's way too out there on the distribution of things that could be productive."* [01:14:20–01:15:57]

### Generalized gremlins (shareable, offered for the repo)

Three gremlin personas pull ideas from a bad-ideas backlog, refine them against their assigned goals, pitch and critique each other, and write a design doc. Hilary explicitly invited people to try it. Hilary: *"Um, but I also extracted a hopefully working, uh, version that anyone can run against their code base. Uh, so maybe we can share that. ## Explainers

### Hamming's science/engineering duality and why agents blur the line
Hilary opens with Richard Hamming's classic principle: in science, if you know what you're doing, stop; in engineering, if you don't know what you're doing, stop. She sees agents as bringing science back into engineering and vice versa. Hilary: *"I feel like this moment where using agents in software development, using it to build all sorts of things, uh, is bringing the science back into the engineering and the engineering back into the science, like what Eric was showing us just earlier, um, in a way that, that is really cool."* The upshot: you can now afford rapid experimentation in domains (product, creative) where iteration used to be too slow to be worthwhile. [00:43:25–00:44:03]

### Agents unlock context-switching for scattered schedules
Personal rather than technical, but foundational to her practice. Hilary: *"I'm a CEO. I have a million bad ideas. My schedule is often going this way and that way... now I have the tools to, uh, set something moving, um, and then have it... I can go do something else and I can come back and I can remember where I was and what I was doing, um, without having, without losing the whole thread or the whole idea."* Previously she had to block two and a half days a week to stay in context; now she can scatter work across the day. [00:44:53–00:45:45]

### LLMs are "aspirationally very mid" — and what to do about it
Hilary names the central creative problem with models: they are biased, repetitive, and homogenizing. Asking for "a doctor" gives you "a man with maybe an Indian name" — not accidentally offensive, but structurally racist. The fix is not to blame the model but to bring sharp intent and context. Hilary: *"they're aspirationally very mid. Like, they are super biased, they're very samesy... And, you know, you're gonna see a lot of repeats, and it's just not necessarily great. Um, and so in order to get to great, you have to bring a lot of context. You have to be very sharp in what you actually want out of it. I'm a huge fan of, like, the Superpowers plugin... because it brings that rigorous process and sort of, uh, verification into things."* [00:46:28–00:47:14]

### The murder problem: guardrails as creative blockers
Hidden Door spent a year solving how to get characters killed in scenes (narratively necessary for stories like The Crow). Off-the-shelf LLMs refuse or defer endlessly. Hilary: *"your off-the-shelf LLM will not do it. Uh, like we, um, we have a world you can play in the world of The Crow... the idea is that you are murdered in the first scene of the story with your lover, and you come back for revenge, and this is your, like, inciting incident. It's the motivation. But in the very first version of this we made, the LLMs were sort of like, 'Oh, they put the gun close to you,' and the next turn is, 'Closer now.' And they would just keep going, but never... It was like, you know, Zeno's, you know, armory, I guess."* The solution: custom guardrails, content standards, and taking certain decisions out of the LLM's hands. [00:56:20–00:57:15]

### Agentic looping for creative refinement (product strategy)
Hidden Door's new product visualizes the agentic loop (goal → variations → evals → iterate) as a user-facing experience. Rather than asking users to write prompts or understand LLM output, the UI shows them sharp editorial feedback (vision, tone, inspirations) updating in real time as they type. Hilary: *"this is really about bringing an interface to this kind of like agentic, uh, looping around an idea. And you can do this for software, and we often do it with the whole like, 'Here's my goal. You know, let's make some tests and evals, and then let's like let the models go and try and make the software until the test pass.' And then we say like, 'Cool, we did it.' Then you look at it and you're like, 'Okay, maybe you did it. Maybe you didn't do it exactly the way I wanted.'"* [00:58:10–00:58:43]

### Variability as a feature, not a bug
A subtle but important teaching point: different people running the same agentic workflow will get different outputs — not because one person did it "right" and another "wrong," but because they each have a different creative vision. Hilary: *"The point of all of this is to get a really sharp creative take from the person doing the writing, and to really understand that if you do it, if I do it, if any one of us does it, we're gonna get a different output. It's not 'cause one is better or worse, it's 'cause we each have a different idea of what, you know, the creative work should be... And that's a, that's a feature. Like, that variability, uh, is something that we certainly bring."* [01:11:48–01:12:00]

### Why multi-gremlin critique generates better ideas
The gremlins work because they have different optimization targets (aesthetic perfection, user delight, product vision) and they argue with each other before committing to a design doc. Hilary: *"There is a perfectionist, which is like make our code base and our product amazing, but like perfect. And then there's the sort of player, the user perspective of like, what would a player want? They sort of pull off this bad ideas list. They make their own spin on it based on the other goals they've been given, and then they propose, they pitch to each other, they critique each other, and then they write a design doc."* [01:15:57–01:16:32]

### Nightshift for scheduled grunt work vs. gremlins for moonshot exploration
A teaching moment about how Nightshift (auto-run linting, outdated test cleanup) is the wrong fit for Hilary's workflow. What she built instead: gremlins that hunt for wild ideas in the Bad Ideas backlog and force them to survive cross-functional critique. The philosophical reframe: *"I love the idea of like, okay, it's the end of the week, I have some tokens. Um, but what I want is to work on the weird stuff, like all the bad ideas, all the stuff that, like, nobody has the time for because the, it's way too out there on the distribution of things that could be productive."* [01:14:20–01:15:01]
